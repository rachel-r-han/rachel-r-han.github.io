# Material Proximity & Tactile Imagination


## Research Question


How do artifacts, replicas, and restoration practices shape human connections with the past?


---


# Research Perspective


Historical objects create unique relationships between people and time.


Unlike written records, physical objects carry material traces of the past. Their texture, preservation condition, distance from visitors, and methods of display influence how people imagine historical experiences.


Through field research, I examined three forms of material encounter:

- protected originals
- interactive replicas
- conservation practices


These different approaches reveal how materiality influences historical intimacy.



---


# Material Encounter 01


## Originals Behind Glass


### Preservation and Distance


Authentic artifacts are often protected through strict conservation measures.


However, protection also creates physical distance between visitors and historical objects.



---


## Sword of Goujian — Hubei Provincial Museum


At the Hubei Provincial Museum, the Sword of Goujian is displayed under strict protection.


Visitors move through the exhibition space in a controlled flow, with limited time for observation.


I stood before the sword for less than thirty seconds.


Although the physical encounter was brief, the experience continued beyond the gallery.


After leaving the museum, I searched for additional historical information about the artifact.


The restriction of touch did not eliminate connection.


Instead, distance encouraged imagination.



---


# Material Encounter 02


## Touchable Replicas


### Reproducing Access


Replicas create another form of relationship between visitors and historical objects.


Although they do not possess the authenticity of original artifacts, they allow physical interaction and emotional engagement.



---


## Silver-Inlaid Bronze Ox Lamp — Nanjing Museum


At Nanjing Museum, I encountered a replica of the Silver-Inlaid Bronze Ox Lamp.


I reached out and touched the surface of the replica.


Although I understood that it was not the original artifact, the tactile experience changed my understanding of the object.


The replica provided a different pathway toward historical imagination.


Instead of replacing authenticity, reproduction expanded access.



---


## Eastern Han Stone Bixie — Luoyang Museum


At the Luoyang Museum, a replica of an Eastern Han stone bixie allowed visitors to interact physically with the object.


Children touched the surface while exploring its form.


In this moment, historical connection was created not through distance and protection, but through participation.


Closeness shifted from visual appreciation to embodied experience.



---


# Material Encounter 03


## Restoration Practice


### Heritage Through Responsibility


Restoration provides another way of approaching historical objects.


Unlike museum visitors who observe artifacts from a distance, conservators interact directly with material traces of the past.



---


## Bronze Ding Conservation Experience


During my internship at the Beijing Leshi Cultural Relics Center, I participated in the cleaning process of a Spring and Autumn bronze ding.


Using a bamboo tool to carefully remove corrosion, I experienced the fragility of historical materials.


Every small action required patience and precision.


The process changed my understanding of conservation.


Artifacts do not survive automatically.


They continue to exist because generations of people dedicate time and responsibility to their preservation.

# Research Insight


Material objects do not simply represent history.


They actively shape how people experience and understand the past.


Glass creates protection but also distance.


Replicas provide access while transforming authenticity.


Restoration reveals that heritage survives through continuous human care.



The relationship between people and history is therefore not only visual or intellectual.


It is also physical and emotional.



---


# Fieldwork Reflection


Before conducting this research, I often viewed museums as places where historical objects were displayed.


Through these encounters, I gradually realized that heritage experiences are shaped by the relationship between people and materials.


The question is not only what an artifact represents, but also how people are allowed to encounter it.


Preservation, accessibility, and participation must exist in balance.


The future of heritage depends not only on protecting objects, but also on creating meaningful connections between objects and communities.
